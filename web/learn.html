Why patterns appear in pure noise

GHOST/USD is made of random numbers. Yet after a minute of watching, most people see trends, double tops and lines the price "respects". This page shows why, with small experiments you can run yourself.

Randomness for these demos

These demos use your browser's randomness (crypto.getRandomValues); the live tape uses the ESP32. A fixed seed replays the exact same charts, so you can share an example.

1Every candle is a coin flip

Each price step is one packet of three random numbers. Two of them become a bell-curve draw: usually a small move, sometimes a big one, up or down with equal odds. About six steps make one 1-second candle.

There are no hidden moods, trends or interest rates in this tape: every step is the same fair draw, with no drift. Nobody picks the direction, not even us.

3Trendlines always fit

A computer drew the "best" support line (under the price) and resistance line (over it) on the first part of this random chart, the way a chartist would: through swing points, never cut by a close.

Join two of the many little swing points and a third one often lands near the line. It feels like proof. It is just what wiggly lines do. After the dashed divider you can see what the price did next.

4The strategy lie detector

A backtest is "what would this rule have earned on past prices". One good backtest feels convincing. So let's run a rule on 1,000 fresh random sessions, through the same demo-account maths as the terminal (1 lot per trade on the $10,000 demo account, spread 0 as configured).

To keep it honest we also run a coin-flip trader: it trades at exactly the same moments, but picks buy or sell by tossing a coin. A rule only has skill if it beats the coin.

The lesson: a great backtest on one chart proves nothing. Out of 1,000 random charts, one will always look like genius. The only fair test is many fresh charts, against a no-skill baseline, with real costs.

5Why GhostTape uses hardware noise

A software random generator is a formula. Feed it the same starting number (the "seed") and it replays exactly the same numbers. That is handy for repeatable examples like the fixed seed above, but it means someone could know the next move.

The live tape uses the random-number hardware in an ESP32 chip, fed by radio and thermal noise. Espressif, the chip maker, says these numbers are truly random only while the chip's Wi-Fi or Bluetooth radio is on. So our firmware switches the radio on, proves it is running with a Wi-Fi scan, re-checks every 30 seconds, and sends no numbers at all if the check fails.

An honest footnote: statistical tests can only show "no pattern we could detect". The "truly random" claim rests on the physical source.
